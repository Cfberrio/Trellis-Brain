*   **System 1 vs System 2 focus and decision-making scaffolds (the "ladder" approach)** Decision
    *   Consensus formed around emphasizing System 2 (slow, deliberate thinking) to build foundations that can later become System 1 (fast, instinctive) habits.
    *   Julian described flag football examples: early weeks required teaching fundamentals and terminology (e.g., kids asked "where's the touchdown? What's a turnover? What's the interception?") before higher-order tactics.
    *   Players later showed analytical thinking (e.g., defenders suggested adjusting to one safety and pressing receivers when QB could not throw deep), illustrating System 2 analysis leading to tactical decisions.
    *   Team agreed to frame skill development as a ladder with a problem to solve at each step; avoid jumping from level 1 to level 10.
    *   Trellis tied in perception–action linkage and variability to provoke decision-making in practice rather than repeating the same drills.

*   **Practice design principles: variability, randomization, retrieval, and active wait time** Decision
    *   Trellis argued for touching multiple skills in one practice (not waiting weeks) and revisiting them frequently to increase retrieval, aligning with the book’s premise.
    *   He recommended increasing variability and randomization (e.g., rotate between throwing, movement lines, game-play segments) rather than massing one topic.
    *   Active wait time was highlighted: increase think ratio and mental effort during downtimes (e.g., simple footwork on sideline) so all players remained engaged.
    *   Cross-sport example (tennis): simulate game context by involving a net player for volleys while others drive forehands; use ladders of progressively harder problems even within a single skill (e.g., forehand).

*   **Desirable difficulty calibration**
    *   They discussed setting a challenge level that was motivating but not overwhelming, ensuring problems at each ladder step were achievable and fun.
    *   Trellis emphasized modulating difficulty appropriately per team and player; avoid skipping several steps or pushing challenges that make players feel inadequate.

*   **Positive framing and solution orientation in coaching communication**
    *   Julian advocated focusing on positive reinforcement and what to do (solutions) versus lists of don’ts, to reduce overload and rigidity.
    *   Sebastian shared journaling insights about prevalent negative self-talk; he argued coaches could promote positive self-talk through their framing, potentially benefiting mental health.
    *   Trellis referenced the book "Soundtracks" by John (author name not fully captured) about mental "songs" and neural pathways; he stressed authenticity in coaches’ own soundtracks to model positivity.
    *   They connected positive framing to making problem-solving appealing; presenting challenges with enthusiasm would increase player engagement.

*   **Feedback and questioning: Check For Understanding (CFU) and think ratio**
    *   Sebastian noted CFU as a major shift in his coaching: breaking segments cleanly and verifying comprehension (e.g., thumbs up/hands up) before progressing increased flow.
    *   Trellis planned to share CFU techniques efficiently during training so coaches appreciated importance, even if imperfect at first.
    *   They linked CFU with pacing: when coaches moved at "100 miles an hour" while players were at "10," CFUs were critical to realign.
    *   Think ratio and cold call were mentioned as techniques to increase mental effort and engagement during practice.

*   **Cross-sport translation of concepts**
    *   Sebastian asked how to generalize System 1/2 and problem-solving to tennis, volleyball, and other sports.
    *   Trellis provided tennis examples (forehand to volley, blocking at the net, rally realism) and emphasized constant coach–player dialogue to build critical thinking across fast games.
