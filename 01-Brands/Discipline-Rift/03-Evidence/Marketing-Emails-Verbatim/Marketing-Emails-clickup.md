Hi Name,

What if this was the season your child did not just participate…
But discovered something about themselves?

The first time they understand a new skill.
The first time they try again after struggling.
The first time they feel like they belong in a group.

Many children stop participating in organized activities before middle school... Not because they lack ability, but because they did not feel supported or capable early on.

Here are five simple ways you can strengthen your child's experience starting this week.

1. Start Every Week with Curiosity
After each session, ask open ended questions:
What felt good today?
What was challenging?
Can you show me something new you practiced?
Curiosity builds reflection. Reflection builds growth.

1. Create a 10 Minute Ritual
Set aside ten minutes twice a week to move together. The goal is connection, not correction. When children associate structured activity with bonding, they stay engaged longer.

1. Celebrate Small Wins
Notice effort, not just results.
Trying again. Staying focused. Listening well.
Confidence grows when progress is seen.

1. Let Them Teach You
Ask your child to explain something they learned. Teaching strengthens understanding and reinforces self belief.

1. Talk About Growth
Have conversations about perseverance, teamwork, and character. These are the qualities that last long after any season ends.

Structured programs can accelerate this growth when they are developmentally appropriate and focused on confidence before competition.

# Editing Principles (Sept 5, 2026): What Luis Changes and Why

Built from the line-by-line edits on the volleyball registration emails, cross-checked against the revision trail in ALL REVISIONS. Use this before writing parent-facing copy so the first draft already looks like the corrected draft.

---

## The 8 principles behind the edits
### 1. The CTA goes where the interest peaks, not where the argument ends
A REGISTER button was inserted immediately under the hero, before the letter even starts. A second one landed right after the one line that does the persuading.

**The thinking:** a parent who is already sold at line three should not have to read eleven more lines to find the door. Placement follows the reader's intent curve, not the writer's outline. The email is no longer a pitch that earns a click at the end. It is a door with an explanation next to it.
### 2. Two sentences become one, and the pivot carries the meaning

| Before | After |
| ---| --- |
| Your child does not need to already be athletic. They have never had to touch a volleyball before.<br>They need a team to begin and develop on. | Your child does not need to already be athletic, but they need a team to begin and develop on. |

**The thinking:** three separate statements let a reader agree with the first and stop. One sentence with a _but_ forces the whole idea through in a single breath. The objection and the answer arrive together, so there is no gap to escape through. Same pattern as the earlier note that "change" beats "shift" and "doing" beats "showing": if the shorter form still lands, the longer form was padding.
### 3. Give the parent agency, do not remove their job
The biggest edit in the email was one word:
> **You never have to ask.** → **You can ask.**
**The thinking:** "You never have to ask" tells a parent their instinct to check in is a problem being solved for them. "You can ask" hands them access. One frames the parent as a recipient of updates, the other as someone with a direct line to their child's coach. Second one is stronger because the dashboard is a permission, not a replacement for parenting.

Same logic killed "without going through admin staff." That phrase describes an internal org chart. A parent does not care what they are bypassing, only that the coach answers.
### 4. Claim what is prepared, not what is promised

| Before | After |
| ---| --- |
| We teach the pass, the set, the serve, until a child who has never touched a volleyball understands it. We drill it in a format they will actually stay engaged in. Then we play a real game using it. Without a method, your child's development comes down to luck. | Our coaches have been training and preparing to coach right. |

**The thinking:** the long version is a curriculum walkthrough written for someone evaluating a program. The short version is a statement about the people. It is also honest at scale: across 55 campuses you can guarantee that coaches were trained, and you cannot guarantee that week three covered the set. Every specific claim is a promise someone has to keep on a Tuesday afternoon.

This is the same instinct as the earlier note on the technical volleyball script: ground it in the real curriculum or do not go technical at all.
### 5. Cut manufactured urgency, keep real timing
"Volleyball rosters close as each school fills" was deleted. "The long weekend ends Monday" stayed.

**The thinking:** the first is a scarcity mechanic the reader has seen a thousand times and cannot verify. The second is a fact about the reader's own week. Real timing pressures better than invented scarcity, and it costs no credibility.
### 6. Drop interface instructions, use the real action
> Click below, search for your child's school, and register. → **Take advantage, and search for your child's school**
**The thinking:** "click below" narrates a mechanic the button already communicates. "Search for your child's school" is the actual thing the parent does, and it is the standard DR parent CTA. It also plants the expectation before they land on the site, so the page does not surprise them. Consistent with the long-running push for CTAs that name a concrete action instead of a generic gesture.
### 7. Reframe the hours as family hours
> the hours between dismissal and dinner → the hours between dismissal and dinner **with family** start clocking
**The thinking:** "between dismissal and dinner" is a scheduling gap. "Dinner with family" makes it the window before the part of the day the parent actually values, and "start clocking" gives it a running meter. The stakes stop being logistical and become personal.
### 8. "Team" everywhere "program" or "court" used to be
"Photos from the court" became "photos from the team." "A place to begin" became "a team to begin and develop on."

**The thinking:** a program is something a parent buys. A team is something a child belongs to. Belonging is what closes, so the noun does the selling.

---

## Positioning: the one edit that needs a decision

The proof line changed from **"Running at 55 schools across Central Florida"** to **"Largest sport program in schools across Central Florida, teaching passion and discipline."**

The reasoning is clear and it is a real upgrade in ambition: a number invites comparison shopping, a category claim ends it, and "passion and discipline" says what the program is _for_ rather than how big it is.

The tradeoff is that it trades a verifiable fact for a superlative. The 55-schools line is the currently sanctioned proof point and it survives in the footer. Two ways to keep the positioning without the exposure:

1. **Pair them.** "The largest sport program in Central Florida elementary schools. 55 campuses, teaching passion and discipline." The number becomes the evidence for the claim instead of competing with it.
2. **Narrow the category.** "Largest after-school sport program in Central Florida elementary schools" is a much more defensible claim than "largest sport program."

Either way, get the superlative confirmed once and then it is sanctioned proof like anything else.

---

## Send strategy: Email 1 and Email 2
The season starts the week of **Monday, September 7 (Labor Day)**, so the two emails are not variants. They are a two-beat sequence.

|  | Email 1 | Email 2 |
| ---| ---| --- |
| Send | This weekend | Labor Day, Monday Sept 7 |
| Job | Introduce the team, remove the objections | Force the decision |
| Angle | "Volleyball starts this week." | "Volleyball, or the tablet." |
| Audience posture | Relaxed, planning the week | Home, off work, last free day |

**Why Labor Day is the strongest send slot of the season.** It is the only weekday all season when the parent is home, not working, and the child is in the room. The screens-versus-team contrast is not an abstraction on Labor Day, it is happening on the couch six feet away. And it is the last day before the season starts, so the deadline is genuine rather than manufactured.

**Three things Email 2 needs for that slot:**
1. **Tighter timing language.** "Starts this week" is Saturday phrasing. On Monday it should be "tomorrow," which is the sharpest true urgency available and needs no invented scarcity.
2. **Shorter body.** Labor Day reading happens on a phone, one-handed, in a distracted house. Email 1 argues; Email 2 should mostly point.
3. **The CTA at the top.** Same principle as edit #1, applied harder. A parent who has already read Email 1 does not need the argument again, only the door.

# Editing Principles: Round Two — The Labor Day Edits

Round one sharpened the argument. Round two changed the posture, and did it without dropping a single conversion element. Numbering continues from the round-one principles doc in MARKETING.

---

### 9. Warmth before urgency. Always.

| Before | After |
| ---| --- |
| Subject: Volleyball starts tomorrow. | Subject: Happy Labor Day! Volleyball is here |
| Preheader: Last day of the long weekend. Tomorrow the team starts without them or with them. | Preheader: Last day of the long weekend. First day of the season is this week. |

**The thinking:** on a holiday, a deadline in the subject line is a company interrupting a family day. A greeting is a person acknowledging it. The urgency did not disappear, it moved one line down where it costs nothing.

And **"without them or with them" was deleted outright.** That is a guilt mechanic. It implies the season happens to the child rather than for them, and it makes the parent the one who might fail. It converts on the first send and costs trust on every send after.

Also: "is here" beats "starts tomorrow." _Here_ is arrival. _Tomorrow_ is a countdown, and countdowns imply someone is already late.

---

### 10. Ask the parent a question. Do not narrate their life to them.

| Before | After |
| ---| --- |
| Look at what your child is doing right now. | Happy Labor Day! What is your family doing today? |
| Tomorrow they go back, and the afternoons fill themselves in. | Tomorrow they go back, and what happens after school? |

**The thinking:** "Look at what your child is doing right now" tells a parent to inspect their kid and implies they will not like the answer. It is a trap dressed as an observation.

The question version does more work with less pressure. A parent who answers "what happens after school?" in their own head has just made the argument themselves, and nobody argues with a conclusion they reached on their own. **Persuasion by question, not by verdict.**

Note the second shift: **"your child" became "your family."** On a family holiday, the unit is the family.

---

### 11. Name the category, not the object
> The **tablet** is not a plan. → The **screens** are not a plan.
**The thinking:** "the tablet" is one household's device. A parent whose kid is on a Switch, a phone, or the TV reads right past it. "Screens" includes everyone, and it matches the vocabulary already running through the DR content pillars, so the email and the organic content reinforce each other instead of sounding like two different companies.

Specificity is a tool, not a virtue. **Be specific about what we do. Be general about what the parent is living.**

---

### 12. Lead with what we provide, not what the child lacks

| Before | After |
| ---| --- |
| Your child does not need to already be athletic, but they need a team to begin and develop on. | We have the space where your child can begin and develop. |

**The thinking:** the round-one line was still built around a deficiency, even while reassuring the parent about it. Saying "does not need to already be athletic" plants the idea that athleticism is the bar and their child is under it.

The new line never mentions the bar. It states what exists and who it is for. **"Space"** is the sharpest word in it: not a program, not a roster, a space being held for their child. The "team over program" instinct from round one, taken one step further.

---

### 13. The child's agency beats our service
> They get **taught**, not just **watched**. → They get **to learn**, not just **spending time**.
**The thinking:** two upgrades in one line.

"Get taught" is something done to a child. **"Get to learn"** is something the child gets to do, and _get to_ carries a small note of privilege. The subject of the sentence moves from us to them.

Then the competitor gets named honestly. "Watched" implies the alternative is bad supervision, which reads as an attack on other programs and on parents. **"Spending time"** names the real thing: after-school hours that pass without producing anything. That is the actual competition, and it is nobody's fault, which is exactly why it is safe to name.

---

### 14. If the subject line said it, the hero should not repeat it
> Last day of the long weekend. / **Volleyball starts tomorrow.** / Get your child on the team today.
> → **Time to get your child on the team.**
**The thinking:** the subject line and preheader already delivered the holiday and the timing. Repeating them in the hero spends the reader's best attention on information they already have.

Treat the subject line, preheader, and hero as **one continuous sentence**, not three chances to say the same thing. Round one put a CTA on the first screen; round two cleared everything out of its way so it lands harder.

The caption "Search for your child's school" under that first button came out for the same reason: the button says REGISTER, the site does the searching, and the promo band repeats the instruction later anyway. One idea per screen.

---

## What the two rounds add up to
Round one was **structure**: CTA early, claims compressed, anything unkeepable at 55 campuses cut.

Round two was **relationship**: greet before you push, ask instead of tell, describe the child as capable, never make a parent feel late.

Both rounds cut roughly a third of the words. Neither cut a single conversion element. That is the tell: the shortest version was always available, the first draft just had not found it yet.

Every line has to survive being read by a tired parent who is not looking for a reason to be told they are doing it wrong.

- [ ] CTA in the first screen, before the letter starts
- [ ] Subject, preheader, and hero say three different things
- [ ] Questions where a statement would have told the parent what to think

- [ ] No line that implies the parent is late or failing
- [ ] Child framed as capable, never as lacking
- [ ] Category words for the parent's life, specific words for ours
- [ ] No specific delivery promise that 55 campuses cannot all keep
- [ ] No scarcity that cannot be verified; use calendar facts instead
