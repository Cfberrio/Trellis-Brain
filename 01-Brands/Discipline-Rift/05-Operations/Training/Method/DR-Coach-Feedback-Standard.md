## Purpose

Coach communication needs to be direct, specific, and tied to the DR method.

We should not soften important coaching corrections with vague language such as:

- “Everything is great.”
- “Just keep doing your best.”
- “Try something different.”
- “Maybe switch it up.”

If something in practice is not following the method, we need to say exactly what is happening and what the coach needs to do differently.

---

## 1. Teach Before You Test

We cannot get deep into practice without having taught skills.

Players should not be asked to demonstrate, compete, serve, rotate, or execute positions before the coach has actually taught the skill or structure required.

Do not assume players know something because:

- They say they know it.
- They are older.
- They have played before.
- One or two players can demonstrate it.

Players will sometimes say “yes” because they do not want to look bad.

The coach still needs to verify understanding and teach.

### Communication Standard

Be direct:

> “You cannot ask players to execute a skill that has not been taught yet.”

> “Teach the form first. Then give them repetitions. Then progress the activity.”

---

## 2. The Method Is Already Figured Out

Coaches do not need to invent their own approach to DR practice.

The approach, teaching method, progression structure, and practice philosophy already exist.

The coach’s responsibility is to:

1. Learn the method.
2. Understand the progression.
3. Plan the practice using it.
4. Apply it correctly.

Experience coaching somewhere else does not replace learning the DR method.

A high school practice approach cannot simply be transferred to elementary developmental players.

### Communication Standard

Be direct:

> “You do not need to figure out the approach. The approach and method are already built. Your job is to learn it and apply it.”

---

## 3. Do Not Let Activities Become Block Practice

If one activity runs too long without progression, practice turns into repetition over and over.

That becomes block practice.

We are designing practices to be serial and progressive.

Players need repetition, but repetition should not become the same exact task indefinitely.

### Communication Standard

Do not say:

> “Switch to something else.”

Say:

> “The activity has run long enough. Add the next layer of variability so players continue progressing.”

---

## 4. Add Layers of Variability

Progress does not mean changing the entire activity.

You do not need a brand-new drill every few minutes.

Players need enough repetitions to understand and stabilize the skill.

Then the coach should add a layer of variability.

Examples:

- Same pass, new target
- Same skill, increased distance
- Same target, player now moves first
- Same pass, less predictable toss
- Same isolated skill, now connect the next skill
- Same activity, now add a decision

The base activity remains recognizable while the challenge increases.

### Communication Standard

Be specific:

> “Keep the activity, but add the next layer.”

> “Do not change everything. Keep the skill recognizable and increase the challenge.”

---

## 5. Fight Against Forgetting

A practice should be designed so players repeatedly retrieve and apply what they have learned.

If they repeat one identical action for too long, they may perform it in that moment without building the ability to adapt it.

The coach should keep moving players through layers of the same learning objective.

That means:

- Teach
- Repeat
- Add variability
- Reapply
- Connect
- Progress

---

## 6. Use Observable Corrections

Coach feedback should point to something that can be seen.

Examples:

Instead of:

> “Practice needs more structure.”

Say:

> “By 3:35, players were serving without having been taught serving form.”

Instead of:

> “Players seemed confused.”

Say:

> “Players were told to get into positions without the positions being taught or checked.”

Instead of:

> “The drill went too long.”

Say:

> “The activity stayed the same for too many repetitions without adding a new target, movement, decision, or connected skill.”

Specific feedback gives the coach something they can fix.

---

## 7. Keep the Focus on Application

Coach communication should always lead back to what the coach needs to do at the next practice.

The goal is not to criticize for the sake of criticizing.

The goal is:

- Identify what happened.
- Tie it to the DR method.
- State the correction.
- Explain what application should look like next time.

---

# Communication Formula

Use this structure when correcting a coach:

### 1. What happened
State the observable behavior.

### 2. Why it matters
Connect it to player learning or the DR method.

### 3. What the method requires
State the correct principle.

### 4. What to do next
Give a specific application.

Example:

> “Players were serving before serving form had been taught. We cannot test a skill before we teach it. Start by teaching the movement, give players controlled repetitions, and then progress into serving over the net.”

---

# Core Standard

**Do not blend or soften the method until the correction becomes unclear.**

If a coach is not teaching, say that.

If an activity has become block practice, say that.

If the coach is inventing an approach instead of applying the DR method, say that.

The communication should be respectful, but it should also be specific enough that the coach knows exactly what needs to change.
