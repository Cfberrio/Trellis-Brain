Pickleball Lesson Plans for Schools

Todd Skezas | September 14, 2025

Pickleball in schools is a pretty cool way for physical education teachers to give their students a taste of movement, skill building, teamwork, and strategy all at once. Widely described as one of the fastest-growing sports worldwide, it gives students a chance to play pickleball while developing speed, coordination, and strategic thinking. The sport has been around since the 1960s, when it was first played in a backyard, and it's great for PE because kids can pick it up and start playing without having to learn a million pickleball rules right off the bat.

This guide is aimed at elementary, middle, and high school PE teachers who are looking for some ready-to-go lesson plans and a clear idea of how to assess their students. We'll explain why pickleball is such a great fit for school PE programs, give you a rundown of three different grade-level lesson plans, and provide some rubrics for measuring technique, participation, teamwork, safety, and strategy. The goal is to help schools build a practical pickleball program that teaches the basics of pickleball, key skills, and safe training without requiring a huge amount of time, space, or money.

Key Takeaways

Pickleball can support cardiovascular activity, agility, endurance, coordination, focus, and quick decision-making.

The smaller court and basic rules can help students with different skill levels participate together.

Portable nets, indoor pickleballs, beginner paddles, tape, and cones can support flexible indoor or outdoor setups.

Elementary lessons should emphasize paddle control, forehand and backhand shots, court awareness, cooperation, accuracy, and simple rallies.

Middle school lessons can add serving skills, volleying, dinking, positioning, the two-bounce rule, pickleball scoring, and basic doubles strategy.

High school lessons can introduce lobs, overheads, drop shots, poaching, strategic patience, and gameplay analysis.

Assessment should measure technique, movement, strategy, engagement, sportsmanship, teamwork, safety, and rule adherence.

Why Pickleball Is Perfect for School PE Programs

Physical, Social, and Mental Benefits

Pickleball is a fantastic way to get kids moving - they're sprinting, shuffling, changing direction, adjusting speed, and recovering all the time. And because it's a bit like chasing a ball around a court, it's a great way to build cardiovascular fitness, agility, and endurance without feeling like you're stuck in some boring old conditioning drill.

The sport is also a great team-building activity. The smaller court and pretty straightforward rules make it easy for kids with different levels of experience to get involved. And when it's doubles, teamwork is crucial - players have to communicate, cover each other's backs, and use strategic thinking to come up with a winning plan.

As students play, they have to watch the ball through the air, control the paddle, read each shot, and decide how to respond. It's a great way to develop hand-eye coordination, concentration, and quick thinking on your feet. And because it's such an active, social pickleball game, it's a great way to build confidence and blow off some steam during a busy school day.

Easy to Set Up and Play

All you need to get started is some portable nets, indoor pickleballs, some beginner paddles , and a safe place to play. The good news is that courts can be indoors or out, and if you don't have a permanent court, you can just use some tape or cones to mark out the space. Different types of layouts can work, and the court distance can be shortened for younger students. Because the rules are pretty simple, teachers can adjust the setup to suit their class size, available access, and space.

Pickleball is a great way to get kids moving and active, and because it's so much fun to play, it's a great way to get them away from screens and into the action. Schools across the country can adapt the activity to fit a gym, blacktop, or outdoor court.

Ready-to-Use Lesson Plans for Different Grade Levels

These lesson plans are all designed to be about 45 to 60 minutes long and are tailored to teach the basics to kids of different ages and skill levels. The progression starts with basic paddle control, moves on to the pickleball serve, serving skills, pickleball scoring, and basic rules, and finishes with more advanced shots and tactics. You can adjust the stations, court size, and rotations to suit your class and the space you have available. And don't forget to start every session with a warm-up and a quick safety and rule review.

Elementary School (Grades 3-5): Learning the Basics and Having a Ball

Objective: Get students practicing a basic paddle grip with their dominant hand, simple forehand and backhand shots, ball control, court awareness, and cooperation.

Warm-Up (10 minutes) : Get the kids moving with a group jog, then add a tag activity where they have to bounce a soft ball on the paddle while moving around. This gets their heart rate up and helps them get used to the paddle.

Skill Instruction (15 minutes): Divide the kids into small groups and rotate them through a few stations every three to four minutes. One station has them tapping a ball with forehand and backhand shots using a hammer grip and flat paddle face. Another has them balancing the ball on the paddle while walking a line. Teachers can also add targets at different distances so students can focus on aiming and accuracy. Short rallying drills help students accumulate consecutive hits, while repeated practice begins building muscle memory before they move on to rally play.

Main Activity (20 minutes): Set up some simplified mini-games on half-courts, and leave off the no-volley rule so the kids can focus on getting the ball in play and working together as a team. Mark out some boundaries with cones if you need to. The main goal is to help the kids play pickleball cooperatively, not to win a game.

Cool Down and Reflection (5-10 minutes) : Get the kids stretching, and ask them to think about what helped their team work together best - was it communication, control, or something else?

Middle School (Grades 6-8): Building Skills and Bringing Out the Strategy

Objective: Get students to develop serving skills, volleying, dinking, positioning, and basic tactical awareness through drills and small games.

Warm-Up (10 minutes): Get the kids moving with some dynamic stretches, then have them practice a controlled underhand pickleball serve with a partner. To spice things up, add a second round where the ball is released without added force, allowed to bounce, and then struck as a drop serve.

Skill Instruction (15 minutes): Teach the underhand serve from behind the baseline, and get the students to practice directing it diagonally into the service area. Introduce dinking as hitting a soft, controlled shot that lands in the opponent's kitchen, and get the students to practice controlled dinks near the kitchen line. To keep it interesting, have the students rotate through serve practice and dink rallies while keeping their knees bent and shots controlled.

Main Activity (20 minutes): Get the kids playing some doubles on a full court, but don't forget to enforce the two-bounce rule. The serve must bounce on the receiving side, and the return must bounce on the serving side before either team can hit the ball out of the air. This lets them get into some real rallies and gives them time to position themselves and work out a strategy. To mix things up, have them rotate partners every few points so they get used to working with different people and adjusting their strategy accordingly.

Cool Down and Reflection (5-10 minutes) : Get the kids stretching, and ask them to think about one strategic thing they did during the game - was it moving with their partner, or placing a soft shot into open space?

High School (Grades 9-12): Taking It to the Next Level

Objective: Get students to refine their technique, apply advanced strategies, evaluate gameplay and use feedback to improve their performance.

Warm-Up (10 minutes) : Get the kids moving with some dynamic stretches, then have them practice some underhand serves with a partner. And to keep it interesting, add a second round where the ball has to bounce before the player makes contact.

Skill Instruction (15 minutes): Teach some more advanced shots like the third-shot drop, the overhead smash, and the angled volley. When practicing overheads, students should move into position and contact the ball at the highest comfortable point they can control rather than reaching wildly. To keep it real, have the students practice these shots in a game situation and get feedback from their partners.

Main Activity (20 minutes): Get the kids playing some advanced doubles on a full court, but don't forget to enforce the rules. This lets them get into some real rallies and gives them time to position themselves, control the speed of play, and work out a strategy. Have players learn how responsibilities can change when they are on the right or left side, and rotate partners every few points so they get used to working with different people and adjusting their strategy accordingly.

Cool Down and Reflection (5-10 minutes) : Get the kids stretching, and ask them to think about one strategic thing they did during the game - was it moving with their partner, or placing a soft shot into open space?

Warm-Up (10 minutes) : Before the real action starts, we'll run a quick circuit that combines jogging with some shadow swings and footwork drills. This circuit will help improve agility and get the students into the rhythm of the game. Shadow swings are great because they let students practice their stroke mechanics without the distraction of chasing after a ball.

Skill Instruction (15 minutes): We'll review overhead smashes and lobs, talk about how a lob can move opponents away from the net, and then dive into mid-court drop shots into the non-volley zone. I want to introduce some poaching techniques to the doubles players, where one player crosses over to take a shot, and make sure everyone understands the importance of court awareness and communication.

Main Activity (20 minutes): Now it's time to play a full doubles match to 11 points while practicing standard pickleball scoring, with the winners getting to decide who rotates in for the next point. Occasionally, I'll call out during a rally and say we need to get in a certain number of dinks before we can try to end the exchange. The goal is to show the kids that sometimes it's better to build points rather than going for a winner every time.

Cool Down and Reflection (5-10 minutes) : We'll start off with some light stretching, then have the players partner up and share one thing they're doing well and one thing they need to work on. The feedback should be about actions they can see on the court, like positioning, shot choice, communication or control.

Assessment Rubrics to Measure Progress

When it comes to assessing the students' progress, we're not just going to look at the final score or how many points they win. We'll be looking at their technique, effort, movement, strategy, teamwork, sportsmanship, safety, and how well they follow the rules. We'll use a four-point scale - 1 means they need improvement and 4 means they're doing an excellent job.

Skills-Based Rubric

Serving and Basic Strokes: If they get a 4, they've got a solid underhand serve and their forehand and backhand shots are looking strong. A 3 is pretty good too - they're serving reliably and their shots are effective, even if they're not consistent. A 2 is where things get a bit more basic - they've got the form but not much accuracy. And if they get a 1, they're really struggling with placement, control, and consistent contact.

Court Positioning and Movement: If they get a 4, they're anticipating the action, moving smoothly around the court and covering their doubles partner well. A 3 means they're in good positions most of the time and their footwork is pretty solid, but they might make a few mistakes. A 2 means they're not quite as quick on their feet and they're not covering the court very well. And if they get a 1, they're stuck in one place, don't anticipate the action and barely move.

Strategy Application: If they're a 4, they're using dinks, lobs and drop shots to good effect and they've got a clear plan in mind. A 3 means they're using tactics pretty well, but there's room for improvement. A 2 means they're mostly just hitting the ball, with no real strategy in mind. And if they get a 1, they're just playing it by ear and not really thinking about the game.

Participation and Attitude Rubric

Effort and Engagement: A 4 means they're consistently participating, cheering each other on and having a great attitude. A 3 means they're active and positive most of the time, but maybe slip up occasionally. A 2 means their effort is a bit hit-or-miss. And if they get a 1, they're not really putting in the effort or they're being a bit of a disruptor.

Sportsmanship and Teamwork: A 4 means they're showing great respect for the rules, communicating effectively and being gracious winners and losers. A 3 means they're being pretty good about following the rules and working together, but they might need a reminder or two. A 2 means they're having some trouble cooperating. And if they get a 1, they're having a lot of problems with sportsmanship and teamwork.

Safety and Rule Adherence: A 4 means they're being super careful about their positioning and following the rules to the letter. A 3 means they're generally safe and rule-conscious, but might slip up now and then. A 2 means they're constantly needing to be reminded about safety and the rules. And if they get a 1, they're not paying attention at all and are being a real safety risk.

How to Use the Rubrics

The key is to use these rubrics across multiple sessions, not just based on one game. By doing that, you can really see the development in their form, confidence, movement and teamwork, and give them specific feedback they can use to improve for the next lesson.

Tips for Successful Implementation

You don't need to turn your whole facility into a pickleball court - a bit of tape and some cones can make a temporary court, and you can even borrow equipment from a local club if you need to. Coaches can also use the wealth of free teaching resources available through governing bodies and community programs. Before schools spend money, they can check whether grants or local partnerships can improve access to paddles, nets, and different types of balls. Plus, you can make adjustments to the rules to make things more accessible to students with different abilities. And if you're getting a bit bored, try mixing up the drills with some short games - it's a great way to keep everyone engaged.

The best way to do this, honestly, is to keep things simple. Start with the basics - control first, then add rules and strategy. Repetition in well-designed pickleball drills can develop muscle memory, but the drills still need a clear purpose and enough variety to keep students focused. That way, you can make sure your beginners are getting a clear path forward while still giving more advanced students enough challenge.

Final Thoughts and Next Steps

Pickleball is a great way for school PE programs to develop movement, coordination, teamwork, and tactical awareness in their students, and it's super accessible too. Just start with a grade-level plan that matches your class, adjust the courts to fit the space you have available, and use the rubrics across several sessions. That way, you can be sure everyone's getting the chance to learn and improve without losing the fun of the game.

FAQs About Pickleball in Schools

Why Is Pickleball a Good Fit for School PE?

Pickleball combines movement, agility, coordination, decision-making, communication, and teamwork. It is also widely recognized as one of the fastest-growing sports, and its smaller court and straightforward rules can make participation more approachable for different skill levels.

What Equipment Do Schools Need for Pickleball?

A basic setup uses portable nets , indoor pickleballs , beginner paddles, and a safe playing area. Tape and cones can mark boundaries when permanent courts are unavailable, and teachers can adjust the distance between boundaries for younger students or smaller spaces.

How Long Should a Pickleball PE Lesson Last?

The plans are designed for 45 to 60 minutes and include a warm-up, skill instruction, a main activity, and a cool-down with reflection.

Can Pickleball Be Taught Without Full Courts?

Yes. Teachers can use half-courts, side-by-side courts, taped boundaries, cones, stations, and simplified mini-games to match the available space and class size.

Which Skills Should Elementary Students Learn First?

Elementary students should begin with grip, paddle control, forehand and backhand contact, court awareness, simple rallies, and cooperation. They should learn to hold the paddle comfortably in their dominant hand and can use targets to improve focus and aiming. Add complex rules after they gain confidence.

How Should Middle School Students Practice Strategy?

Middle school students can practice underhand serves, volleys, dinks, positioning, doubles communication, and the two-bounce rule. A dink is a soft shot designed to land in the opponent's kitchen, usually from near the kitchen line. Rotating partners helps students learn to adjust their movement and communication.

What Should High School Pickleball Lessons Emphasize?

High school lessons can emphasize overheads, lobs, drop shots, poaching, patient dink exchanges, full doubles play, and analysis of shot selection and court positioning. Students can also practice taking the ball at a controlled highest point on overheads and understanding responsibilities on the right and left side.

How Can Teachers Assess Student Progress?

Teachers can use a four-point rubric to track serving, strokes, movement, strategy, engagement, sportsmanship, teamwork, safety, and rule adherence across several sessions. The score should reflect progress over time, not just the outcome of one pickleball game.

What Basic Pickleball Rules and Scoring Should Students Learn?

Beginners should learn the pickleball serve, the two-bounce rule, the non-volley zone, and basic pickleball scoring. Under standard USA Pickleball rules, the serve travels diagonally into the opposite service court, only the serving team can score a point, and games are commonly played to 11 points with a two-point winning margin.

Why Should Players Return Serves Deep?

Players should focus on returning serves deep into the opponent's court. A deep return keeps the serving team farther from the kitchen line, creates more time for the returning team to move forward, and can help the return team maintain its positional advantage.

How Do Pickleball Drills Help Beginners Improve?

Pickleball drills develop muscle memory through repetition when students repeat sound movement and stroke patterns. Target drills can sharpen focus and aiming skills, and rallying drills help students accumulate consecutive hits. Teachers can begin at a short distance and gradually move students farther away as their control and accuracy improve.

Do Students with Racquet Sports Experience Have an Advantage?

Students with a background in tennis or other racquet sports may have an early advantage because grip awareness, tracking, footwork, and forehand and backhand shots can feel familiar. They still need to learn pickleball-specific rules, serving technique, kitchen play, and control at a slower speed.

Does a Drop Serve Have to Be Hit Below the Waist?

No. The below-the-waist requirement applies to the volley serve, not the drop serve. For a legal drop serve, the player releases the ball from a natural, unaided height without adding force or spin, lets it bounce, and then strikes it. Teachers should use the current USA Pickleball rulebook when teaching serving rules.

Can Schools Use Sports Premium Funding for Pickleball?

Eligible primary schools in England may use PE and Sport Premium funding for additional and sustainable improvements to PE, sport, and physical activity. A pickleball program may qualify when it is additional to ordinary core curriculum provision and meets the current grant conditions, such as through sustainable staff training, broader access, or extracurricular opportunities. Schools should review the guidance before deciding how to spend the funding.

What Did PHIT America's Published School Pickleball Kit Include?

PHIT America's 2023 PLAY PICKLEBALL launch described a school equipment and curriculum package that included 24 paddles, 30 indoor pickleballs, 30 foam balls, a net, and training aids, giving participating schools across the country a more complete starting setup. Schools should confirm the contents of any current version before applying.
