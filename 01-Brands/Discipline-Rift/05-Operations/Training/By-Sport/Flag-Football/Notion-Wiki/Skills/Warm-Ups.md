# WARM-UP BLOCK — Movement Preparation & Practice Primer

## Time Allocation
- **Attendance + Purpose Talk:** 3–5 minutes
- **Movement Warm-Up:** 3–5 minutes
- Total Opening Block: **5–10 minutes maximum**

Warm-ups should be efficient, intentional, and directly connected to the day's teaching focus. This is not conditioning time. This is preparation time.

## Purpose of the Warm-Up Block
- Set tone and expectations
- Introduce movement vocabulary
- Prime the body for the day's skill focus
- Build transferable habits
- Increase focus and energy

We are preparing: the body, the brain, the language, the mechanics.

## Who Leads the Warm-Up?
**Head Coach** — leads attendance and purpose; sets tone; introduces vocabulary cues.
**Assistant Coaches** — demonstrate movements; correct technique; reinforce cues.

Early in the season, coaches lead. Later in the season, players may help lead for ownership and leadership development.

## Warm-Up Structure (Standard Format)

### 1. Attendance & Purpose (3–5 min)
- Take attendance.
- State the objective of practice.
- Connect today's focus to previous learning.

> "Today we're working on cutting and changing direction. That means we need fast feet and strong hips."

Keep it short and clear.

### 2. General Movement Activation (1–2 min)
Light locomotor movements: high knees, karaoke, lateral shuffle, quick jog.
Goal: raise heart rate, activate lower body, build rhythm.

### 3. Dynamic Stretching (1–2 min)
Keep stretches dynamic and brief: reach one arm overhead; cross arm across body; switch sides; overhead elbow pull; shoulder rolls.
Purpose: loosen shoulders and hips, prepare joints for change of direction. Not static stretching. Not extended.

### 4. Skill Primer Movement (2–3 min)
This is the most important part. Movements must connect directly to the day's drills.

## Core Movement Progression
When teaching cutting, defending, or route running, players move through:
1. **Quick Feet**
2. **Step**
3. **Pivot**

Sequence: "Quick feet!" → "Step!" → "Pivot!" Both directions. Repeat 4–6 reps each side.

**What each step teaches**
- **Quick Feet** — deceleration, balance, body control
- **Step** — planting the outside foot, weight transfer
- **Pivot** — hip rotation, direction change

This becomes automatic language during drills and games.

## Vocabulary Transfer Rule
In practice or games, instead of explaining mechanics, use cue words.
> "FOR!"

Players immediately execute: Quick feet → Step → Pivot, because it has been repeated in warm-up. This builds fast reaction and shared language.

## Connecting Warm-Up to Drill Theme
- **Route Running** → hip turn; plant and drive; break mechanics
- **Defensive Containment** → breakdown steps; lateral shuffle; controlled pivot
- **Flag Pulling** → low hips; short choppy feet; controlled stop

Warm-ups must preview drills.

## Coaching Language During Warm-Up
Use short cues only: "Quick feet." / "Low hips." / "Step." / "Pivot." / "Explode."
Do not stack instructions. Repetition builds clarity.

## Optional Warm-Up Variations
Warm-up may rotate between: movement progression block; Lines Game (field awareness); 4-Corner movement variation. Structure remains: **Activate → Stretch → Skill Primer**.

## Curriculum Note
Drills listed in the curriculum system are tools, not scripts. Coaches should:
1. Identify skill goal.
2. Identify movement breakdown.
3. Select or modify drill to address it.
4. Keep warm-up aligned with that skill.

Add creativity — but maintain purpose.

## Example 5-Minute Warm-Up Flow
- Minute 0–3: Attendance + Purpose
- Minute 3–4: High knees → Karaoke → Quick feet
- Minute 4–5: Quick feet → Step → Pivot, both directions

Efficient. Focused. Connected.

## Final Philosophy
Warm-ups are not filler. They set culture, build vocabulary, prime mechanics, reinforce fundamentals, and save teaching time later. You are not just warming up muscles — you are warming up habits.
